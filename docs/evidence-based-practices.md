## MBSR: does the benefit require lifelong practice?

Short answer: less than the meditation-industry narrative implies.

**The key study:** ["Staying Well: A Follow-Up of a 5-Week Mindfulness Based Stress Reduction Programme for a Range of Psychological Issues"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10597-014-9825-5) (*Community Mental Health Journal*). N=28 (of an original 149-person referred sample), average age 50, referred for a range of chronic psychological issues. Participation significantly improved well-being immediately post-training, and **that improvement was maintained up to 4 years later — with continued practice frequency showing an insignificant relationship to well-being scores at follow-up.** People who kept a daily formal practice weren't doing meaningfully better than people who'd let it lapse.

Caveats worth holding onto: this was a **5-week** program (a shortened, non-standard variant of the usual 8-week MBSR), the follow-up sample is small (N=28), and it's one study, not a meta-analysis. Treat the *direction* of the finding as reasonably solid (it's echoed elsewhere, below) but the specific numbers as low-powered.

**Corroborating evidence, weaker dose-dependence than expected:**
- [Parsons et al. (2017), meta-analysis of MBSR/MBCT home practice and outcome](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5501725/) — across 28 studies, home practice during the 8-week program correlated with outcome only modestly (r = 0.26). More practice helps, but it's not a steep dose curve — you don't need to hit the full prescribed dose to get most of the benefit.
- A 2025 *Scientific Reports* study found that state changes in mindfulness *during* an intervention **do not predict trait changes** in mindfulness, self-compassion, or perceived stress afterward — suggesting durable change comes from something more like a qualitative shift in how you relate to your own thoughts, not from accumulated practice-minutes.
- A 6-year longitudinal RCT in Norwegian medical/psychology students found mindfulness training produced a lasting reduction in **neuroticism** across the full 6 years — a genuine trait-level shift, not a fading state effect.
- Counter-evidence / where it doesn't hold: a veterans-focused review found MBSR's effects on **PTSD symptoms specifically did not persist** at follow-up the way general depression/mindfulness effects did. So durability looks stronger for general mood/stress/trait shifts than for treating something acute or severe — under real strain, the "installed" skill likely needs active re-engagement, not autopilot.

**The foundational MBSR evidence base**, for calibration: [Goyal et al. (2014), JAMA Internal Medicine](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/1809754) — meta-analysis finding small-to-moderate improvements in anxiety, depression, and pain, but **no evidence these programs beat other active treatments** (exercise, CBT, health education). MBSR is a solid tool, not a uniquely superior one. [Kuyken et al. (2015), PREVENT trial, The Lancet](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(14)62222-4/fulltext) — MBCT matched (not beat) maintenance antidepressants for depression relapse prevention over 24 months.

## What else actually has evidence, ranked

A quick audit of the other "industry-standard" self-improvement practices, same rigor applied.

### Social connection — the strongest finding on this list, and probably underrated

[Holt-Lunstad et al. (2015), meta-analysis, N > 3.4 million](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1745691614568352) — loneliness and social isolation predict mortality with an effect size (OR ≈ 1.26–1.32, i.e. 26–32% increased risk) **comparable to smoking** as a risk factor. This isn't a psychological nicety, it's one of the best-replicated findings in the whole behavioral-health literature, and it gets far less popular attention than meditation or journaling.

### Exercise — strong in general, but be skeptical of the flashy recent numbers specifically

The widely-cited [Noetel et al. (2024), BMJ network meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11131084/) reported large effects for walking/jogging (g ≈ −0.62), yoga (g ≈ −0.55), and strength training (g ≈ −0.49) against depression. **Treat those specific numbers with real caution**: the paper has an issued correction, and independent reviewers found coding errors (including at least one implausible effect size), data that couldn't be reproduced from the authors' own sources, and rated most of the underlying evidence "very low quality." This is the same pattern as Polyvagal Theory and *The Body Keeps the Score* elsewhere in this repo — a genuinely important-sounding result that doesn't fully survive scrutiny.

The more conservative, better-supported footing: exercise for mood is one of the more replicated findings in the field going back well before Noetel (Cochrane-level reviews, Schuch et al. 2016), just with less dramatic effect sizes than the 2024 headline numbers suggest. Public-health dose guidance (WHO/CDC: 150–300 minutes/week moderate-intensity aerobic activity) is the safer number to anchor to than any single modality-specific claim.

### Expressive writing / journaling — real, small, cheap

[Frattaroli (2006), meta-analysis of 146 disclosure studies](https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/faculty.sites.uci.edu/dist/c/602/files/2019/08/Frattaroli-psych-bulletin-2006.pdf) — overall effect size r = .075 across physical, psychological, and overall functioning outcomes. Small, but statistically real, highly variable by context, and essentially free — Frattaroli's own conclusion is that it's worth doing for its cost-effectiveness, not because it's a strong intervention on its own.

### Gratitude journaling — smaller than its popular reputation

[Cregg & Cheavens (2021), meta-analysis, k=27, N=3,675](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10902-020-00236-6) — small effects on depression and anxiety specifically, small-to-medium on general well-being. The authors' own recommendation: don't rely on gratitude interventions as a primary treatment for depression or anxiety — use evidence-backed treatments for that, and treat gratitude practice as a minor supplement.

### Sleep — foundational, not really a discrete "intervention"

Not its own effect-size literature in the same sense as the above, because it's closer to a precondition than a technique: 7–9 hours for most adults, well-established downstream effects on mood regulation and stress reactivity when chronically short. Worth naming because it's the thing most likely to quietly undercut all of the above if neglected.

## Rough ranking, evidence-strength-only

1. **Social connection** — largest, most-replicated effect of anything here
2. **Sleep** — foundational, not optional, understudied as an "intervention" only because it's assumed
3. **Exercise** — genuinely strong in the broader literature; be skeptical of the specific 2024 modality rankings
4. **MBSR / mindfulness training** — solid, roughly on par with other active treatments, appears to produce some durable trait-level change without requiring lifelong maintenance
5. **Expressive writing** — small, real, essentially free
6. **Gratitude journaling** — smallest and most overstated relative to its popular reputation; fine as a supplement, not a treatment
